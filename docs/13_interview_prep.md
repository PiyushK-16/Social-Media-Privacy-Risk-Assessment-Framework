# Interview Preparation (Section 46) - 10 questions and answers

**1. Explain your project.**
I built the Social Media Privacy Risk Assessment Framework, a defensive tool that estimates privacy risk from self-reported settings. A user answers 44 choice-based questions; I validate them, convert answers into risk values, compute ten category scores and a weighted overall score from 0 to 100 where higher means more exposure, and classify it as LOW, MODERATE, HIGH or CRITICAL. It then generates ranked findings, prioritised recommendations, a what-if simulator, a dashboard on 1,000 synthetic profiles and a printable report. It never scrapes or tracks anyone and stores only scores and finding types.

**2. What is the difference between privacy and security, and how does your project show it?**
Privacy is about how much personal information is exposed and used; security is about protecting accounts and systems from unauthorised access. My questionnaire separates them: categories like location and personal information are privacy-heavy, while MFA, password reuse and login alerts are security-heavy. A user can score low on account security risk but high on location exposure, which proves strong security does not mean strong privacy.

**3. What is a digital footprint and how did you handle it?**
It is the trail of data about you: active (what you deliberately post) and passive (data generated by your activity). Since I can't and shouldn't observe real activity, I only score self-reported habits: whether old posts and unused accounts were reviewed, public comments, and how often settings are checked. I explain passive footprint in the docs but don't try to measure it.

**4. How does social engineering relate to your scoring?**
Scammers use trust and context. My social-engineering category scores behaviours such as accepting unknown requests, clicking unexpected links, answering unverified DMs, joining giveaways and sharing verification codes. Public details like employer or travel plans also raise risk in other categories because they make scams more believable. I included awareness content but deliberately no attack scripts.

**5. How does your risk scoring work, and how did you choose the weights?**
Each answer becomes a risk value from 0 to 1 (safe 0, friends-only 0.4, sometimes 0.5, not sure 0.6, risky 1). Inside a category I take a weighted average and scale to 100. The overall score is a weighted sum of categories (for example personal information, location and account security at 15% each). I chose the weights as educational assumptions based on how directly each area affects exposure, made them configurable, and state clearly they would need validation before professional use. I tested the maths by hand and the boundaries 20/21, 40/41 and 70/71.

**6. How did you apply data minimisation?**
In three places: the questionnaire only asks "is it publicly visible?" so I never receive phone numbers or addresses; the server doesn't save raw answers, only scores, finding types, ID and timestamp; and deletion and a 90-day retention purge limit how long even that stays. I wrote a test that scans the database schema and a dump for sensitive columns and a canary string.

**7. Why is MFA important, and how is it reflected in your project?**
MFA means a stolen or reused password alone is not enough to take over an account. It is one of my IMMEDIATE recommendations, rated high severity, and the improvement simulator shows how much the score drops when it is enabled. I recommend the strongest available method, like an authenticator app or security key.

**8. How do third-party applications affect privacy, and what do you recommend?**
Connected apps can keep access after you stop using them, and "sign in with social account" spreads that access. I assess whether apps are reviewed, unused ones remain, and how widely social login is used. The recommendation is least privilege: review connected apps regularly and revoke what isn't needed.

**9. Explain how your project follows Privacy by Design.**
Privacy is built in rather than added later: choice-only input (minimisation), no accounts or platform access (least privilege), nothing stored beyond minimal results (privacy by default), a clear disclaimer and data statement (transparency), a delete endpoint protected by a secret token (user control), retention limits, and secure processing with validation, escaped output, CSP headers and rate limiting. Documented in docs/04.

**10. How did you test it, and what would you improve?**
I wrote 45 automated pytest tests: 33 functional (each exposure creating the right finding, score maths, thresholds, simulator, database, report) and 12 security/privacy (validation, XSS, rate limit, headers, deletion authorisation, retention, no cookies). They all pass and generate a results table. Next I would calibrate the weights with expert review, add platform-specific checklists, replace the in-memory rate limiter with a shared one, and consider a FastAPI and React version.
